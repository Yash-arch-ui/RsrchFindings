# SoK: Security of Autonomous LLM Agents in Agentic Commerce

## Metadata
- Authors: Qian'ang Mao, Jiaxin Wang, Ya Liu, Li Zhu, Cong Ma, Jiaqi Yan (Nanjing University; Southern University of Science and Technology; City University of Hong Kong)
- Date: 1 May 2026 (v2)
- Venue/ID: arXiv:2604.15367v2 [cs.CR]; preprint
- Length: 17 pages, 143 references
- Type: Systematization of Knowledge (SoK)
- Read status: full text read

## One-paragraph summary
This SoK builds a unified security framework for autonomous LLM agents that hold wallets or delegated payment credentials and transact without per-transaction human approval. It organizes threats into five dimensions (agent integrity, transaction authorization, inter-agent trust, market manipulation, and regulatory compliance), derives 12 cross-layer attack vectors, and assesses seven agent-payment protocols and interfaces: ERC-8004, AP2, OKX's Agent Payments Protocol (APP), x402, Virtuals' Agent Commerce Protocol (ACP), ERC-8183, and the Machine Payments Protocol (MPP). The central claim is that agentic-commerce security is inherently cross-layer: compromise at the reasoning or tool layer becomes custody, settlement, market, or compliance harm at another layer. The paper contributes a PRISMA-style corpus, a threat taxonomy, a protocol comparison, and a five-layer defense architecture. It is a synthesis and research agenda; it reports corpus and coding statistics but no attack experiments or benchmark results of its own.

## Problem and threat model
The paper defines an autonomous financial agent as an LLM-powered software system that (1) maintains persistent state including financial assets or payment instruments, (2) independently plans and executes financial transactions, (3) operates without per-transaction human approval, and (4) interacts with blockchains, payment networks, exchanges, and other agents. This excludes co-pilot systems requiring confirmation, fixed-rule algorithmic traders, and advisory chatbots. Four autonomy levels are distinguished: L0 advisory, L1 supervised, L2 delegated, and L3 fully autonomous; the paper focuses on L2 and L3.

The threat model is a taxonomy rather than a single formal game. It considers attackers who can inject prompts through data feeds, smart contract metadata, or inter-agent messages; poison models or memory; compromise tools and plugins; steal or misuse credentials; exploit negotiation and market structure; create Sybil identities; collude across agents; and exploit regulatory gaps. The key structural assumption is that financial transactions are irreversible and human oversight is reduced, so ordinary LLM failures become financial losses. The paper also emphasizes systemic risk from model and protocol homogeneity: many agents share the same foundation model or stack, so one exploit can propagate market-wide.

The corpus was assembled with 23 search phrases in Google Scholar and Web of Science, supplemented by backward snowballing and targeted inclusion of protocol documents, regulatory texts, industry reports, and implementation notes. The database stage produced 1,373 records, reduced to 1,237 after DOI- and title-level deduplication; 37 database works entered the public corpus, 105 additional works were added through snowballing and targeted inclusion, and 1,192 database candidates were screened out. The public corpus is 142 works, spanning 1994-2026. A 30-row blinded replication set was coded by two independent coders; on the 17 rows with non-empty source and target labels, agreement was kappa = 0.850 for source-layer labels, 0.833 for target-layer labels, and 0.871 for the joint ordered pair.

## Architecture / protocol / taxonomy
The taxonomy has five dimensions (D1-D5). D1 agent integrity covers prompt injection (direct data feeds, smart contract metadata, inter-agent communication), model poisoning and backdoors, tool and plugin compromise, and memory injection. The paper extends memory attacks into write-path poisoning, retrieval manipulation, and memory staleness, with provenance tags, trust-weighted memory, and TTLs as defenses. D2 transaction authorization covers credential and key management, spending policies and bounds, and intent verification. D3 inter-agent trust covers identity and authentication, negotiation integrity, collusion and coordination, and cascading compromise via prompt infection. D4 market manipulation covers agent-driven manipulation, adversarial herding, and sandwich attacks. D5 regulatory compliance covers KYC/AML for non-human actors, VASP classification and principal liability, liability attribution, audit trails, and an obligation-to-control mapping.

The 12 cross-layer vectors are: P2T Prompt-to-Transaction (LLM to blockchain; injected prompt triggers a signed transaction); T2R Tool-to-Reasoning (false data poisons a decision); A2M Agent-to-Market (LLM bias exploited in negotiation); R2I Reg-to-Integrity (regulatory gap enables laundering); T2T Tool-to-Transaction (tool modifies transaction parameters after reasoning); P2K Prompt-to-Key (injection bypasses the key-custody boundary); M2A Model-to-Authorization (backdoor defeats a spending-policy check); C2E Collusion-to-Escrow (colluding evaluators drain escrow); O2P Oracle-to-Position (cumulative drift via subtle feed manipulation); I2M Identity-to-Market (Sybil trust enables coordinated manipulation); N2C Neg-to-Compliance (structuring payments evades AML thresholds); and S2I Supply-to-Integrity (a backdoored plugin silently alters transactions). The paper distinguishes T2R from T2T (corrupt reasoning versus corrupt execution after correct reasoning), T2R from O2P (acute versus chronic), and P2T from P2K (new unauthorized action versus coerced signing). P2T, T2R, T2T, and S2I are called the most immediate deployment risks; C2E, O2P, and N2C are slower-burn and harder to detect; R2I and M2A are marked more speculative because of fewer direct-support works.

The protocol comparison covers Virtuals/ACP, ERC-8183, ERC-8004, AP2, OKX APP, x402, MPP, and MCP. ACP operates in four phases: negotiation producing a signed Proof of Agreement, transaction with escrow, evaluation by specialized evaluator agents, and settlement. ERC-8183 defines a Job primitive with Client, Provider, and Evaluator roles and a state machine Open -> Funded -> Submitted -> Terminal, extensible through hooks and integrated with ERC-8004 reputation. AP2 introduces payment intents and separates intent declaration from execution. OKX APP defines charge, escrow, session, and upto intents and a Broker role. x402 embeds a 402 Payment Required challenge-response into HTTP. MPP extends HTTP 402 with a challenge-credential-receipt flow and supports charge and session intents. MCP standardizes tool access. Deployment status is qualified: ERC-8004 and ERC-8183 are drafts with limited deployment; AP2 has no widely adopted reference implementation; OKX APP is newly announced with escrow and dispute features forthcoming; x402 has early Coinbase deployment but is not standardized; MPP has a live Tempo mainnet deployment and is the most operationally mature; ACP is deployed on Virtuals but platform-specific.

## Section-by-section findings
- Section 1 frames the fragmentation across LLM security, blockchain security, FinTech, and multi-agent systems, and lists five contributions.
- Section 2 defines autonomous financial agents, autonomy levels, and the key systems: OpenClaw, ERC-8004, AP2, OKX APP, Virtuals/ACP, ERC-8183, x402, MPP, and MCP. It includes a terminology note disambiguating ACP-Commerce from ACP-Control and Payment Delivery Receipt from Policy Decision Record.
- Section 3 describes the corpus method, selection criteria, PRISMA flow, cross-layer derivation, coding buckets C0-C5, and synthesis dimensions D1-D5.
- Section 4 presents the five-dimension taxonomy with concrete attack subtypes and defenses, plus incident lessons.
- Section 5 compares defense approaches, gives the 12-vector catalog, compares protocols, proposes the five-layer defense architecture, and lists open problems.
- Section 6 concludes that agentic-finance risk is not the sum of traditional financial security and generic LLM security but arises from their interaction under irreversibility and reduced oversight.

## Key mechanisms and equations
There are no equations. The operational content is in named mechanisms and identifiers. The five-layer defense architecture is: Layer 1 prompt and tool hygiene (sanitize inputs, tag agent-originated content, verify tool provenance before invocation); Layer 2 verified execution context (output validation, runtime context binding, capability graphs); Layer 3 payment authorization and custody (separate cognition from custody, scoped spending policies at signing or credential layer, end-to-end binding of payment parameters); Layer 4 inter-agent trust controls (authenticated identity, stake- or reputation-backed evaluator selection, anomaly monitoring for collusion and Sybil behavior); Layer 5 market and compliance monitoring (circuit breakers, cumulative position-drift detection, exposure aggregation, tamper-evident audit trails).

Other exact mechanisms include ACP's four phases and evaluator-agent primitive; ERC-8183's three roles and four-state Job machine; OKX APP's four payment intents and Broker role; x402's HTTP 402 challenge; MPP's challenge-credential-receipt and charge/session intents; and ERC-8004's on-chain spending limits, agent identity, and revocation. The paper notes concrete latency trade-offs for MEV defenses: private RPC endpoints add zero submission latency versus public RPC but 50-200 ms routing delay to next-block inclusion; orderflow auctions add a 300-800 ms auction window; slippage bounds add zero latency but can cause reverts if too tight. It cites FinCEN CTR thresholds above $10,000 and SAR thresholds of $5,000 or more, and MiCA Regulation 2023/1114 and FATF 2021/2023 VASP guidance as compliance anchors.

## Experiments / evaluation
This is a SoK, so the evaluation is corpus-level rather than experimental. The reported quantitative results are the PRISMA counts (1,373 records, 1,237 after deduplication, 37 database works plus 105 snowballed/targeted works, 1,192 screened out, 142-work public corpus spanning 1994-2026) and the inter-coder agreement on the 30-row blinded replication set (17 rows with both coders; kappa 0.850 source, 0.833 target, 0.871 joint). There are no attack success rates, no false-positive or false-negative measurements, no latency benchmarks, and no comparison against a baseline defense in a testbed. The protocol comparison in Table 4 is qualitative, marking which dimension each protocol addresses and where it is N/A. The paper explicitly calls for finance-specific benchmarks because AgentDojo and ASB omit finance-specific attack classes and TraderBench and CAIA do not fully integrate inter-agent trust and compliance vectors.

## Paper-specific findings
1. No analyzed protocol covers all five dimensions; payment protocols improve authorization, settlement, or coordination, while MCP contributes tool-access control and auditability but is not a payment protocol.
2. The most dangerous attacks are cross-layer: a prompt injection at the LLM layer can become a signed token transfer, and a compromised tool can alter transaction parameters after correct reasoning.
3. Evaluator-mediated escrow (ACP/ERC-8183) introduces a new trust primitive and a new attack surface; the paper names four evaluator attacks: bribery, Sybil evaluator clusters, evaluator-provider collusion, and adversarial evaluator substitution.
4. Per-agent authorization cannot prevent adversarial herding because each individual action may be within scope while the aggregate is harmful; market-level circuit breakers and model-diversity requirements are needed.
5. Memory injection is treated as a persistent, cross-session threat distinct from prompt injection, with write-path poisoning, retrieval manipulation, and staleness subtypes.
6. AP2's intent separation, MPP's digest-bound requests, and ERC-8004's on-chain limits are complementary, not substitutable.
7. The paper argues that "a human can intervene," "prompt injection is a localized bug," and "on-chain finality implies correctness" are invalid assumptions for autonomous agents.
8. Systemic risk from shared foundation models and execution stacks is underappreciated; a single exploit can propagate across a homogeneous agent population.
9. Protocol maturity varies; MPP is the most operationally mature, while AP2 and OKX APP escrow/dispute features are proposals or forthcoming.
10. Compliance is not separable from security: AML structuring (N2C) and Travel Rule provenance requirements map directly to authorization and inter-agent trust controls.

## Limitations and caveats
The paper's own limitations are implicit in its evidence classification: the corpus mixes peer-reviewed work with protocol documents, industry reports, and incident evidence; some vectors (R2I, M2A, C2E) have fewer direct-support works and are marked speculative; there is no formal threat model or empirical validation; protocol status is a snapshot of early 2026 and may age quickly; and the five dimensions are an organizing framework, not a proof of completeness.

Additional caveats: the PRISMA count of 142 works is a corpus size, not a measure of evidence quality. The kappa values apply only to 17 rows of a 30-row set, so they are a reliability check on a small replication sample, not on the whole corpus. The protocol comparison is qualitative and does not score implementation quality; its claims about coverage are only as good as the seven protocols selected. The market-manipulation and compliance sections lean on regulatory interpretation that may not hold in every jurisdiction.

## Hackathon hooks
What it enables: a ready-made threat checklist for any agentic-commerce demo. The 12 vectors can be used as test cases, and the five-layer defense maps cleanly onto a demo architecture: sanitize inputs, bind execution context, separate signing from reasoning, authenticate counterparties, and log cumulatively. The evaluator-attack list is directly useful for designing an escrow demo with bonded evaluators and VRF selection. The protocol comparison helps position a Zcash/Monad project against AP2, x402, MPP, and ACP without overclaiming.

What it does not enable: it does not provide a reference implementation, a benchmark harness, or empirical security guarantees. It does not give a concrete privacy design for shielded settlement, and it does not evaluate Zcash. It does not prove that the five-layer defense works; the layers are proposed, not tested.

Demo ideas: (1) a cross-layer attack replay where a poisoned tool result changes the recipient in a signed payment, with a Layer 3 binding check that blocks it; (2) an escrow demo with three evaluators, stake, slashing, and a simulated collusion attack; (3) a cumulative-spend monitor that catches a sequence of individually valid payments; (4) a Zcash/Monad mapping showing which of the 12 vectors are mitigated by shielded amounts versus which remain at the metadata layer.

## Claims to avoid
Do not claim the SoK proves any attack works at scale; it is a synthesis. Do not claim all 12 vectors are equally grounded; the paper itself flags R2I and M2A as more speculative. Do not claim any protocol is secure or insecure in absolute terms; the comparison is dimension-by-dimension and snapshot-dependent. Do not claim MPP is standardized or that AP2 has a widely adopted implementation. Do not claim the five-layer defense has been evaluated. Do not treat the 142-work corpus or the kappa values as a systematic review of all agentic-commerce security.
