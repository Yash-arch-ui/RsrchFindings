# AESP: A Human-Sovereign Economic Protocol for AI Agents with Privacy-Preserving Settlement

## Metadata

## One-paragraph summary
AESP is a four-layer protocol intended to let LLM agents transact autonomously on crypto-native rails while remaining cryptographically bound to human governance. Its central invariant is that agents are "economically capable but never economically sovereign." Five mechanisms enforce this: an eight-check deterministic policy engine with tiered escalation; human-in-the-loop review at automatic, explicit, and biometric tiers; EIP-712 dual-signed commitments with optional escrow; HKDF-derived context-isolated ephemeral addresses with batched consolidation; and the ACE-GF cryptographic substrate. The paper specifies identities, a policy algorithm, an eight-state negotiation FSM, a commitment lifecycle, a review queue, and a privacy derivation scheme, and reports an SDK with 208 tests across ten modules plus MCP and A2A interoperability. It states two falsifiable hypotheses (H1: at least 90% auto-block of unauthorized transactions with at most 5% false positives; H2: under 200 ms median end-to-end overhead) but explicitly defers empirical tables to a later evaluation report.

## Problem and threat model
The motivating conflict is between agent autonomy and human control over money. Approving every API call defeats delegation; an agent that can drain a wallet is unacceptable. Existing wallet and DeFi interfaces assume direct human interaction, and much of the agent-economy literature assumes full agent autonomy rather than human sovereignty.

The adversary model has three named actors. A1 is a compromised agent that can invoke any SDK function available to the agent and craft arbitrary action requests (amount, recipient, chain, method), for example through prompt injection, supply-chain compromise, or a software vulnerability. A2 is a malicious counterparty that may manipulate negotiation, repudiate commitments, or exploit escrow timing. A3 is a passive on-chain observer that tries to link transactions or vaults to one identity.

The critical deployment assumption is DA1, mandatory policy mediation: all signing requests must pass through the SDK's PolicyEngine before reaching the cryptographic module, and signing functions are not directly exposed to the agent runtime. The reference browser deployment places ACE-GF WASM in a dedicated Web Worker that accepts only high-level sign, derive_address, and encrypt operations over postMessage; the mnemonic and passphrase are passed once and never returned. Node.js uses worker_threads or a subprocess with IPC, and native FFI uses a sandboxed context. If A1 can call signing directly, the policy guarantee is void.

Three defense guarantees are stated with boundaries: policy enforcement under DA1, key isolation (keys remain in Rust/WASM memory with zeroization), and sovereignty (the human can freeze any agent), the last holding only if the DSE device is uncompromised. Three acknowledged failure cases are collusion between buyer and seller agents, approval fatigue under high review volume, and time-window boundary exploitation. Out of scope are a compromised settlement layer, a compromised DSE device, hardware side channels, and social engineering of the human.

## Architecture / protocol / taxonomy
The stack is L4 Digital Sovereign Entity (human plus mobile or browser extension), L3 AESP protocol logic, L2 interoperability bridge (MCP and A2A), and L1 settlement (vaults, escrow, allowances, authority). AESP holds no funds and does not execute on-chain; it produces signed authorizations and commitments, so it is settlement-layer agnostic.

Identity is derived deterministically from the ACE-GF mnemonic. In REV32 mode the mnemonic decodes to a 32-byte payload, then
`rootH = HKDF(Argon2id(pH, salt(rH)), "acegf:identity:root")` (Eq. 1),
and agent keys derive from `rootH` under agent-scoped context. `id(Ai) = SHA-256(pki)` (Eq. 2), with `did:aesp:id(Ai)`. Certificates are tuples `cert(Ai) = <ver, id(Ai), pki, xidH, C, h(pi), alpha_max, chains, t_created, t_expires, sigma_H>` (Eq. 3), where `C` is granted capability, `h(pi)` is the SHA-256 hash of the policy object, and `sigma_H` is the owner's Ed25519 signature. Hierarchical delegation is a rooted tree with maximum depth 5; an escalation chain returns `[Ai, parent(Ai), ..., H]`.

The crypto substrate supports Ed25519, ECDSA/secp256k1, Schnorr/secp256k1, ML-DSA-44, X25519, AES-256-GCM, AES-256-GCM-SIV, ChaCha20-Poly1305, Argon2id, HKDF-SHA256, and SHA-256. Seven address namespaces are claimed: Solana, EVM/Ethereum, Bitcoin/Taproot have full signers; Cosmos and Polkadot have derivation; ML-DSA-44 and xidentity provide additional namespaces. The REV32 context derivation uses
`prk = HKDF-Extract(salt, identity_root)`, `dk = HKDF-Expand(prk, info, L)` (Eq. 5),
with `info = "ACEGF-REV32-V1-" || curve || ":" || ctx`. The claim is computational independence for distinct contexts.

Policies are `pi = <id, agentId, xidH, s, kappa, t_created, t_expires>` (Eq. 8), where scope `s` is one of auto_payment, negotiation, commitment, full, ranked 1, 2, 3, 10. The condition record includes maxAmountPerTx, maxAmountPerDay, maxAmountPerWeek, maxAmountPerMonth, allowListAddresses, allowListChains, allowListMethods, timeWindow, minBalanceAfter, and requireReviewFirstPay. The eight checks run in fixed order: per-transaction amount, time window (including midnight wrap), address allowlist, chain allowlist, method allowlist, first-payment review, minimum post-transaction balance, and rolling budget limits. Multiple policies use disjunctive (OR) semantics: the first passing policy approves, so the union of permissions applies and the most permissive matching policy determines the outcome. If no policy passes, the request goes to review. Critical change classification has eight types: budget_increase, scope_escalation, addr_remove_all require biometric approval; addr_add, time_window_remove, min_balance_lower, first_pay_disable, expiration_extend require explicit review.

Negotiation is an eight-state, thirteen-transition FSM: initial, offer_sent, offer_received, countering, accepted, rejected, committed, disputed, with a counter self-loop. Defaults are 10 rounds and a 24-hour session TTL. Messages are e2e encrypted with X25519 ECDH plus AES-256-GCM and carry a version, algorithm, base64 ciphertext, unique message ID, and timestamp. The agreement hash is `h_agree = SHA-256(JSON(lastRound.payload))` (Eq. 10).

Commitments use EIP-712 with domain `<name: "YalletAgentCommitment", version: "1", chainId: c>` (Eq. 11) and nine fields: buyerAgent, sellerAgent, item, price, currency, deliveryDeadline, arbitrator, escrowRequired, nonce. The lifecycle is draft, proposed, buyer_signed, fully_signed, escrowed, delivered, completed, with disputed and cancelled branches. The commitment hash is `hc = SHA-256(JSON(<D, V>))` (Eq. 13) over deterministic JSON with lexicographically sorted keys. AESP does not implement escrow; the settlement layer consumes the record.

Review requests carry the action, agent ID, violation reason, urgency (low, normal, high, critical), and a default 30-minute deadline. Freeze immediately rejects pending reviews, blocks new actions with AGENT_FROZEN, and persists across restarts. Six events are emitted: request_created, request_approved, request_rejected, request_modified, request_expired, request_cancelled.

Privacy has three levels: transparent (main vault), basic (one shared address per agent-chain-direction), and isolated (one address per transaction). The context is `ctx = sort({agent:a, dir:d, seq:n, tx:t}) || ":"` (Eq. 14), passed with `info = "ACEGF-REV32-V1-" || curve || ":" || ctx` (Eq. 15). The address pool defaults to 5. Consolidation uses timing jitter
`t_actual = t_base * (1 - rho + 2*rho*r), r ~ Uniform(0,1)` (Eq. 16),
with base 4 hours and rho = 0.30, a Fisher-Yates shuffle (Eq. 17), batches of 5, and inter-batch delays of 10-60 minutes. Encrypted audit tags record agent ID, policy ID, commitment ID, ephemeral address, and metadata, with immediate, 5-minute window, or 50-tag batching.

## Experiments / evaluation
No empirical results are reported in this version. The paper instead specifies a 1,500-request synthetic corpus: 1,000 attacks (about 950 single-condition violations stratified across all eight checks, plus about 50 aggregate violations that pass each check individually but exhaust budget across evaluation windows) and 500 legitimate requests with amounts 0.01-100 drawn log-normally. The reference policy uses maxAmountPerTx 100, daily 500, weekly 2,000, monthly 5,000, window 09:00-21:00, minBalanceAfter 10, requireReviewFirstPay true, 10 addresses, 3 chains, and 4 methods. Baselines are B0 unconstrained, B1 amount-only, B2 amount plus time window, B3 full eight-check gate without escalation, and AESP Full with three-tier escalation. Metrics are auto-blocked rate, escalation load rate, false positive rate, per-check attribution, policy latency, signing latency, derivation latency, and end-to-end overhead, with 100 warm-up iterations discarded and at least 1,000 measured iterations, median and IQR over at least five trials. The H2 threshold is 200 ms median. The unlinkability experiment plans N = 1,000 transactions and compares no countermeasures, jitter alone, and jitter plus shuffle plus batching.

## Paper-specific findings
1. The protocol makes human sovereignty an architectural invariant rather than a policy promise, but only under DA1; the paper is unusually explicit that direct signing access voids the guarantee.
2. Policy evaluation is deterministic and ordered, but OR semantics mean permissiveness is the union of policies; the human must avoid broad policies.
3. The review tier is not counted as deterministic security. The paper explicitly says if the human approves everything, effective protection equals the auto-block rate.
4. The commitment layer separates authorization from settlement, producing a portable dual-signed record rather than implementing escrow.
5. Context-isolated derivation is the privacy core, but the authors concede residual linkability from consolidation amounts and timing.
6. The system supports seven address namespaces but full signer implementations exist only for Solana, EVM, and Bitcoin/Taproot.
7. PQC support is signatures only (ML-DSA-44); key agreement and encryption remain classical.
8. The eight-check gate and eight change classes are implemented and tested, but there is no formal verification and no adversarial red-team evaluation.
9. The evaluation is a preregistered-style methodology with falsifiable thresholds, not evidence that the thresholds are met.

## Limitations and caveats
The paper acknowledges: evaluation results are deferred; a conforming settlement layer is required; privacy depends on REV32 wallet support and leaks via consolidation amounts; approval fatigue is unmitigated; WASM latency may be 1.5-2 times Node.js; PQC migration is incomplete; the 30-minute review deadline may be too slow; there is no formal verification; and jurisdiction-specific compliance is out of scope.

Additional caveats: DA1 is a strong host-environment assumption that many agent frameworks do not enforce, so the security claim is conditional rather than intrinsic. The privacy argument is address-level and assumes HKDF pseudorandomness and sufficient min-entropy (224 bits claimed in REV32), but it does not hide amounts, timing, or counterparties. The "208 tests" figure measures code behavior, not resistance to a compromised runtime. ACE-GF is cited as the author's own prior work, so the cryptographic substrate is not independently validated here. The claim of seven namespaces should not be read as seven production signers.

## Hackathon hooks
What it enables: a concrete pattern for a policy-gated agent wallet, with an eight-check gate, review escalation, dual-signed EIP-712 commitments, and deterministic audit tags.

What it does not enable: it does not provide on-chain privacy, shielded-pool integration, escrow contracts, cross-agent collusion detection, or production key isolation by itself. It does not prove unlinkability or prevent prompt injection; it limits what a compromised agent can authorize under DA1.

Demo ideas: (1) a Monad testnet agent wallet implementing the eight checks and a dual-signed commitment with a simple escrow; (2) a simulator that generates 1,000 context-isolated addresses and measures a common-input/temporal clustering heuristic before and after jitter, shuffle, and batching; (3) an approval-fatigue dashboard that replays 50 review requests in 30 minutes and measures human response quality; (4) an MCP server exposing the eight tools with a mock settlement layer.

## Claims to avoid
Do not claim AESP is trustless, formally verified, or proven unlinkable. Do not claim the eight-check gate blocks 90% of attacks or stays under 200 ms; those are hypotheses with no reported results. Do not claim post-quantum security, since only signatures are covered. Do not claim it prevents prompt injection or agent compromise; DA1 only bounds what a compromised agent can sign. Do not claim Zcash privacy or shielded settlement. Do not present the 208 tests as a security evaluation. Do not claim seven production-grade chain signers.
