## One-paragraph summary
This is a first-of-its-kind systematization of blockchain-based agent-to-agent (A2A) payments. It proposes a four-stage lifecycle (discovery, authorization, execution, accounting) as a common abstraction, then maps representative designs onto it along dimensions for discovery substrate, intent-binding primitive, authorization carrier, policy expressiveness, settlement path, submission/fee orchestration, access-gating evidence, verification evidence, and service-payment coupling. It argues that blockchain makes agent payments programmable and verifiable but does not guarantee their correctness: existing systems provide partial guarantees at individual stages and fail to preserve consistency across the end-to-end workflow. Four recurring challenges are identified: weak intent binding, misuse under valid authorization, payment-service decoupling, and limited accountability. The paper closes with directions on consistency, control, composition, and formation. It does not report a PRISMA-style corpus count, experiments, or quantitative evaluation; its evidence is a structured mapping of representative systems and protocols, including x402, MPP, ERC-4337, EIP-2612, Permit2, A402, Skyfire, Kite, EIP-8004, Tempo, and others.

## Problem and threat model
Conventional payment rails assume explicit human initiation and authorization through merchant interfaces. Agent ecosystems instead require programmable, interoperable, verifiable payments without bilateral trust, often at high frequency and low value. Card-network agent payments such as Mastercard Agent Pay allow delegated credentials but process each interaction as an independent authorization, which is costly for frequent small payments and multi-step tasks. Blockchains offer programmability, global accessibility, and transaction verifiability, but introduce latency, fees, scalability, and privacy constraints.

The security problem is not merely "can the agent pay?" but whether payment intent, authorization, execution, and service outcome remain aligned. Once financial authority is delegated, errors, misalignment, or adversarial manipulation can cause direct loss, unauthorized spending, and governance violations. The paper cites evidence that agent protocol stacks expose protocol-logic vulnerabilities and supply-chain attacks, and that payment-enabled workflows amplify prompt injection and interaction manipulation.

The threat model is not formalized as adversary capabilities. Instead, risks are analyzed at three levels for each lifecycle stage: technical, transaction-level, and legal/institutional. The recurring adversaries are a compromised agent or environment (via prompt injection, model manipulation, key leakage, software vulnerabilities, or social engineering), a malicious or non-delivering service provider, colluding agents that distribute actions across identities, and an observer who exploits transparent transaction histories. The paper also treats institutional inadmissibility (jurisdiction, sanctions, licensing, platform policy) as part of the risk surface.

## Architecture / protocol / taxonomy
The lifecycle has four stages. Discovery identifies a payable operation and constructs a structured payment intent binding the obligation to the requested resource, terms, and a stable request identifier. Authorization decides whether an authenticated request is admissible under delegated spending constraints such as budgets, policies, and approvals. Execution realizes the intent as a payment action and produces a completion signal, including handling failures, retries, and settlement delays. Accounting maintains records linking payment actions to originating intents and observed outcomes for reconciliation, audit, and dispute resolution.

The taxonomy uses these codes:
- Discovery substrate: D1 descriptor-based (AgentCards, HTTP metadata, registries), D2 identity-augmented (Kite, EIP-8004-style claims, Skyfire's Know Your Agent layer), D3 behavior-derived (services ranked by payment flows and interaction traces).
- Intent binding: B1 payment-term binding via in-band signaling (x402-style challenge-response), B2 context and provenance binding (identity/session-scoped delegation, on-chain intent proofs).
- Authorization carriers: A1 contract-mediated delegation, A2 allowance/approval-mediated spending (ERC-20 approvals, EIP-2612, Permit2), A3 wallet-mediated programmable authorization (ERC-4337 account abstraction with UserOperation and EntryPoint).
- Policy expressiveness: E1 access-level constraints, E2 transaction-level constraints, E3 contextual and stateful policies (rate limits, cumulative bounds, delegation scopes).
- Settlement paths: S1 direct on-chain settlement, S2 off-chain-coordinated settlement with deferred on-chain finality (Kite, Tempo).
- Submission and fee orchestration: O1 client-submitted transactions, O2 account-abstraction-mediated relaying with bundlers and paymasters, O3 facilitated submission with off-chain authorization (EIP-2612/Permit2 and a facilitator).
- Access-gating evidence: G1 inclusion-gated access (x402), G2 off-chain update-gated access, G3 proof- or attestation-gated access (signed receipts, cryptographic proofs, A402, Acharya/TIVA).
- Verification evidence: V1 on-chain transaction evidence, V2 interaction-level receipts and logs.
- Service-payment coupling: C1 execution-trigger coupling, C2 post hoc accountability linkage (insurance-based), C3 enforcement-based coupling (A402 atomic service channels with adaptor signatures).

Table 1 maps representative designs to the stages they primarily support. The paradigms are service-specified payment (x402, MPP, Vaziry et al., Skyfire, Agent Pay), owner-specified payment (ERC-4337, Dimitrov et al., Gorzny et al.), identity-augmented payment (Kite, EIP-8004), payment execution infrastructure (Circle nanopayments, Tempo, Ravi), and cross-stage coupling (Li et al./A402, Acharya/TIVA). Across these, the paper covers 15 named designs in five paradigms. It does not claim the list is exhaustive.

## Section-by-section findings
- Section 1 motivates A2A payments and states four contributions: the lifecycle model, the design-space mapping, the risk surface, and future directions.
- Section 2 distinguishes LLMs as generative components, agents as orchestrating systems, and blockchains as programmable settlement.
- Section 3 defines the four-stage lifecycle and the participant interaction framework: payable conditions arise at the agent-merchant interface, payments execute via wallet/key management, settlement occurs on-chain, and receipts support reconciliation.
- Section 4 maps existing approaches stage by stage using D1-D3, B1-B2, A1-A3, E1-E3, S1-S2, O1-O3, G1-G3, V1-V2, and C1-C3.
- Section 5 analyzes risks per stage at technical, transaction, and legal/institutional levels, then covers authentication/identity risks and future directions.
- Section 6 concludes that blockchain enables payments but does not inherently guarantee correctness.

## Key mechanisms and equations
There are no equations. The technical content is in named protocol mechanisms. x402 uses an HTTP 402 challenge-response: a service returns payment requirements (asset, recipient, amount), and the agent attaches a payment transaction or proof in a subsequent request. MPP integrates payment authorization and confirmation into API flows and gates access on successful completion. ERC-4337 uses UserOperation objects, an EntryPoint contract, bundlers, and paymasters; validation logic in the smart contract wallet checks signatures, policies, and contextual constraints before inclusion. EIP-2612 provides off-chain signed token approvals; Permit2 provides finer-grained approval control; facilitators can submit transfers and sponsor gas. A402 introduces atomic service channels that bind payment completion to service delivery via adaptor signatures, so payment is finalized only when execution-dependent secrets are released. Skyfire's KYA layer links agent identities and credentials to discoverable services. Kite provides identity-centric, session-scoped delegation and off-chain-coordinated settlement. EIP-8004-style mechanisms provide portable on-chain reputation.

## Experiments / evaluation
There are no experiments, datasets, baselines, or quantitative metrics. The paper is a qualitative systematization: it maps designs to lifecycle stages, identifies risks, and proposes directions. It does not report corpus counts, coding agreement, attack success rates, latency, cost, or throughput measurements. References to latency, fees, scalability, and privacy are presented as known constraints of blockchain payment infrastructures, not as measured results in this paper. The "Table 1" marks primary contribution, discussed, and not covered, but there is no numerical scoring.

## Paper-specific findings
1. Blockchain A2A payment correctness is a cross-stage property; a design can be sound at authorization and still fail at accounting because payment and service delivery are decoupled.
2. Weak intent binding is the first systemic gap: discovery constructs intents from externally exposed metadata without authenticating semantic identity, creating phishing-like surfaces through counterfeit endpoints, typosquatted domains, spoofed AgentCards, or deceptive registry entries.
3. Authorization is a local validity filter over individual transactions, not a mechanism for behavioral correctness over time; sequences of individually valid payments can violate spending boundaries through repetition, fragmentation, or timing, and colluding agents can distribute actions across identities.
4. Execution confirms payment completion, not service completion; in x402-style inclusion-gated models, financial finality does not establish fulfillment, so a provider can receive valid payment without delivering the correct outcome.
5. Accounting links payments to outcomes only loosely, often through separate logs or receipts; causal attribution from payment to agent reasoning to responsible principal is under-specified.
6. Identity authentication is not identity attribution: a system can authenticate a key, account, or endpoint yet remain blind to whether the action came from the intended agent, the correct principal, or a stable identity.
7. Privacy and accountability are in tension: transparent histories improve verification and auditing but expose interaction patterns, counterparties, and timing; stronger privacy reduces evidence for accountability.
8. The paper identifies four future directions: consistency through a shared append-only execution record spanning discovery, authorization, execution, and accounting; control that governs behavior over time rather than isolated transactions; composition across multi-hop, multi-agent, and mixed on-chain/off-chain workflows; and formation of payable terms through iterative negotiation rather than fixed upfront pricing.
9. A402's adaptor-signature atomic channels are the strongest example of enforcement-based coupling, but the paper notes they remain confined to execution-layer coordination and do not eliminate inconsistent interpretations of completion across workflows.
10. Off-chain-coordinated settlement improves throughput but creates asynchronous, locally visible states that cannot be globally synchronized in time, making a single authoritative finality point difficult.

## Limitations and caveats
The paper's own limitations are implicit: it is a conceptual systematization with no formal threat model, no implementation, no empirical evaluation, and no systematic corpus methodology comparable to a PRISMA review. The design space is representative rather than exhaustive, and some cited systems are whitepapers, proposals, or early deployments whose properties may change. The legal/institutional analysis is high-level and jurisdiction-agnostic. The paper treats privacy mainly as a trade-off, without a technical privacy construction.

Additional caveats: the four-stage lifecycle is an analytical abstraction, not a claim that every system implements the stages explicitly or in that order. The claim of being "first" to systematize blockchain A2A payments is time-bound to April 2026. The risk analysis is not backed by incident counts or exploit measurements, and the "weak coupling" finding is qualitative. The paper does not quantify the cost of its proposed shared execution record, nor does it address how such a record preserves privacy. The reference to x402 and MPP should not be read as an endorsement of their production security; the paper itself notes protocol-logic and supply-chain risks in agent stacks.

## Hackathon hooks
What it enables: a clean vocabulary for decomposing an agent-payment demo into discovery, authorization, execution, and accounting, and for showing exactly where a design is strong or weak. The intent-binding distinction (in-band terms versus identity/session provenance) is useful for designing a payment request that cannot be replayed against a different service. The coupling taxonomy (C1-C3) suggests a compelling demo: compare x402-style inclusion-gated access with an A402-style adaptor-signature atomic channel. The accounting gap motivates a tamper-evident receipt or execution trace.

What it does not enable: it does not provide a reference implementation, a privacy protocol, a formal security proof, or a benchmark. It does not specify how to integrate Zcash shielded transactions, and it does not solve the privacy-accountability trade-off it identifies. It does not give concrete smart contract interfaces beyond naming ERC-4337, EIP-2612, Permit2, and A402 concepts.

Demo ideas: (1) an x402-style paid API on Monad with a receipt that binds the request hash to the payment, then a replay attempt that fails; (2) an A402-inspired atomic service channel using adaptor signatures, showing that payment and delivery finalize together; (3) a cumulative-spend monitor that catches fragmented payments across multiple agents; (4) a discovery demo with a spoofed AgentCard and an identity-pinned registry that rejects it; (5) a Zcash/Monad design sketch separating shielded payment from a public service-delivery attestation.

## Claims to avoid
Do not claim the SoK proves which payment design is secure; it is a qualitative mapping. Do not claim A402 or any proposal is deployed or solves coupling end to end. Do not claim x402 provides accountability or service-completion guarantees; it gates access on payment inclusion. Do not claim the four-stage lifecycle is exhaustive or universally followed. Do not claim the paper reports corpus counts, attack measurements, or performance numbers; it does not. Do not claim blockchain settlement eliminates trust; the paper explicitly says it does not guarantee correctness.
